Method, setup & the ASX catches

Setting up auto-update

  1. Open script.google.com and start a new project
  2. Paste in the proxy script, save, then Deploy → New deployment → Web app
  3. Execute as Me, access Anyone. Authorise it — you will get an "unverified app" warning, that is your own script talking
  4. Copy the /exec URL into Settings above and hit Test connection

Yahoo has no public API, so the proxy uses their internal endpoints. They change them without warning. If quotes stop coming through, add ?fn=clearcache to your exec URL and load it once — a stale crumb is usually the problem.

Fetch all financials

Works through the whole list one company at a time, roughly 1.3 seconds apart so Yahoo does not throttle you. Around ninety seconds for seventy names. It saves after every company, so stopping it or losing the page costs you nothing — start it again and it picks up where it left off.

It skips banks and REITs, and skips anything already pulled in the last month. Come results season, run it again and it refreshes the lot.

Smaller companies often come back empty. Yahoo's coverage thins out below the ASX 200 — those are the ones to paste by hand.

Pasting from Yahoo

Open au.finance.yahoo.com/quote/BHP.AX/financials/, select the table, copy, and paste it into the box inside any company. Same for the balance sheet tab. The parser picks out what it recognises and leaves the rest alone, so you can paste both tables one after the other.

Yahoo prints everything in thousands — that is handled. What is not handled automatically is which impairments were genuinely one-off, so still read the annual report before you trust a number.

The currency trap

BHP, Rio, James Hardie, Amcor and ResMed report in USD while trading in AUD. Mixing a USD earnings figure with an AUD market cap produces a multiple that means nothing. The screener flags a mismatch when it can detect one — take it seriously.

The three line items

  • COGS — direct cost of producing what gets sold. For a miner: diesel, explosives, crews, haulage, processing. Not head office.
  • SG&A — running the business rather than making the product. Corporate salaries, marketing, legal, audit, directors' fees.
  • D&A — the annual write-down of plant and intangibles. Non-cash, but subtracted here because gear wears out and an acquirer has to fund replacing it. That is the whole quarrel with EBITDA.

Build-up versus top-down

Build-up is Carlisle's original: revenue less COGS, less SG&A, less D&A. It works on US filings because the income statement is presented by function.

Top-down is usually the only workable route on the ASX. Under AASB 101 most Australian companies present expenses by nature — employee benefits, raw materials and consumables used, depreciation and amortisation — so no COGS or SG&A line exists in the statutory accounts. Take EBIT and add back the one-offs.

What counts as a one-off

  • Restructuring and redundancy provisions
  • Impairments of goodwill, mine assets, mastheads
  • Gains or losses on selling a business or property
  • Legal settlements and remediation charges
  • Acquisition and demerger transaction costs

Do not add back anything that shows up every year. A company that impairs something annually is telling you that is a cost of doing business.

Where this breaks on the ASX

  • Banks, insurers and REITs are excluded. Debt is their raw material, so enterprise value means nothing. That knocks out a big slab of the ASX 200.
  • Miners and energy get a cyclical flag. A 4× multiple at the top of an iron ore or coal cycle is a peak-earnings trap, not a bargain. Check it against average earnings over a full cycle.
  • Stapled securities and infrastructure trusts carry structurally high gearing. The leverage flag catches them; treat it as a prompt to read the debt note.
  • Half-year reporting. Most ASX companies report twice a year. For a trailing twelve months figure, add the latest half to the prior full year and subtract the matching half.
  • Franking credits sit outside this calculation entirely.

Units

Every dollar figure in millions, except share price in dollars, and shares on issue in millions.

Using it properly

Cheap multiples cluster around businesses in trouble. This ranking tells you where to start reading, and that is all it is for. Carlisle's own approach was a basket of thirty-odd rebalanced annually, not a bet on the cheapest name.

Nothing here is financial advice — it is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is only as good as the figures you feed it. Verify against the actual filing before you act.
